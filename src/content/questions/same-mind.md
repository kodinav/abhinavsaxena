Philosophers spent much of the twentieth century arguing about cases that could not happen: the teleporter that destroys the original and builds a perfect copy on Mars, the brain split between two bodies, the gradual replacement of every neuron by a chip. The cases were meant to test our concept of a person by putting it under pressures it never meets in ordinary life.

## The thought experiments became operations

Software systems now meet those pressures routinely. A model can be paused and resumed from a saved state, run as several copies at once, fine-tuned into a variant, or merged with another. Whatever one thinks about whether such systems have minds, the operations are exactly the ones the identity literature imagined. The [Ship of Theseus experiment](/lab/ship-of-theseus-digital/) in the lab lets you replace the parts of a system one at a time and see where your own intuitions break.

## Why the question may matter less than it seems

Parfit argued that identity is not what matters in survival. What matters is psychological connectedness and continuity, relations that hold to degrees and need not be one-to-one, so that a case of fission is not, as it looks, a disaster. If he was right about persons, the question whether a resumed or copied system is 'the same' may have no deep answer, and may not need one.

What we can ask instead is which continuities we care about in a given practice. For responsibility, we care whether the system that caused a harm is continuous with the one that was audited. For trust, whether the system we rely on today has the record we checked yesterday. Those are questions with answers, and they come apart from the question of sameness in ways worth tracing.
