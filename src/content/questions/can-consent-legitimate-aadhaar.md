seoTitle: Can Consent Make Aadhaar Legitimate?

The response to Aadhaar's failures, in the courts, in activism and in much of the scholarship, has been to demand better consent: clearer notice, meaningful opt-out, stronger guarantees for the data subject. India's Data Protection Act of 2023 builds its architecture on consent and consent managers. These demands are not wrong. For a distinctive class of cases they are addressed to the wrong question.

## What consent presupposes

The liberal tradition specifies at least four conditions for valid consent: the person must be competent, informed, acting voluntarily, and someone whose authorisation is normatively relevant, someone with standing over the domain in question. Debates about consent failing almost always concern the first three: the patient without capacity, the user who did not read the policy, the employee who cannot refuse monitoring. The fourth, standing, is almost always taken for granted.

Aadhaar's hardest cases fail on standing. A person can meaningfully consent to enrolment only if she occupies a standpoint from which she could say: I will not enrol, and I will keep my standing as an entitlement-bearer by other means. For the documented urban citizen that standpoint exists, in attenuated form. For the rural labourer without a birth certificate or a stable address, it is not merely costly but illusory. Refusing Aadhaar is not refusing an identification mechanism. It is refusing to exist as a subject the welfare state can see.

## Practical failure and conceptual failure

Consent can fail in two ways. It fails practically when its conditions could be met but are not, as when a privacy policy is too long to read; the fix is better design. It fails conceptually when its conditions cannot be met in that context, when no redesign, however ambitious, could restore it to its role.

Medical ethics already knows the second kind. When a patient lacks the capacity to consent, we do not pretend a better brochure would restore it; we replace consent with surrogate decision-making and best-interests standards. The question about Aadhaar is whether something analogous is required, not because its subjects are incompetent, but because the conditions of their consenting, a standing that precedes the system, have not been met. Where the system produces that standing, consent has nothing to authorise. This is the condition I call [constitutive dependence](/questions/what-is-constitutive-dependence/).

## Three patterns

Three patterns in the Aadhaar record make the point. The labourer whose worn fingerprints fail at the ration shop authorised enrolment, not her own exclusion, and her exit would itself exclude her. The migrant worker's entitlement is tied to a node in a national database, so the movement on which her livelihood depends puts her legibility, and so her food, at risk. And the child enrolled before she can consent to anything has her standing as a recipient of school meals and scholarships produced through the enrolment itself.

In each case the normative question is not whether the system secured valid consent. It is [what an institution owes](/questions/what-do-institutions-owe/) when it is the condition of the recognition its subjects depend on.
