The ethics of AI studies the moral questions raised by building and deploying artificial intelligence and other algorithmic systems: who is wronged by them, what is owed and by whom, and what could make their use legitimate. My work in it starts from a case.

An elderly labourer goes to the ration shop for her monthly grain. Her entitlement is authenticated through Aadhaar, India's biometric identity system, and her fingerprints, worn down by a lifetime of manual work, do not read. The usual response to failures of this kind is to ask for better consent: clearer notice, meaningful opt-out, stronger data protection. In ["Beyond Consent"](/publications/beyond-consent/) (*AI and Ethics*, 2026), which I presented at the Interdisciplinary Speaker Series on the Ethics of AI at IIT Delhi in April 2026, I argue that this misdescribes what has happened. She has not failed to give informed consent. She has failed to be recognised as someone who could give or withhold consent to anything at all.

## Why consent is the wrong tool here

[Informed consent](/glossary/informed-consent/) came into data protection carrying the authority of post-war bioethics, from the Nuremberg Code to the Belmont Report, and it carries much of the legitimating and remedial load in the frameworks that govern algorithmic systems: the GDPR, the EU AI Act, India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. The doctrine was built around a patient whose standing as a rights-bearer was never in doubt. Even its sharpest critics, Solove on privacy self-management and Nissenbaum on contextual integrity, keep that presupposition: a subject given in advance of the system.

I do not claim that consent organises AI ethics as a field, which is plural. The claim is narrower. For [algorithmic welfare systems](/glossary/algorithmic-welfare/), which combine automated decisioning, a criterion enforced without discretion at the point of application, and integration across databases, consent is not merely strained in practice but conceptually misplaced. A practical failure of consent can be repaired by better notice and better design. A conceptual failure cannot be repaired by any redesign, because what is missing is a standpoint from which refusal would make sense.

I call the condition [constitutive dependence](/glossary/constitutive-dependence/). The institution produces a person's operative standing as an entitlement-bearer; no standpoint of refusal preserves that standing; and her recognition can be revised inside the system without appeal to an independent ground. What is produced is not her personhood or her legal status, and the condition is not coercion, instrumental dependence or background inequality. I set it out more briefly in [what constitutive dependence is](/questions/what-is-constitutive-dependence/).

## What is owed instead

If legitimacy cannot come from the authorisation of people whose standing the institution produces, it must come from what the institution owes them. The [Constitutive Obligation Principle](/glossary/constitutive-obligation-principle/) holds that such an institution's legitimacy depends on discharging non-waivable obligations of three kinds.

- **[Legibility](/glossary/legibility/).** Recognition criteria must respond to the material reality of the people they are meant to recognise; a criterion that systematically fails worn hands has to be redesigned.
- **Non-abandonment.** The institution may not withdraw the recognition it produced without meeting a burden of justification.
- **Remediation.** There must be a substantive pathway inside the institution when recognition fails. An online grievance portal, offered to someone the scanner could not read, is not one.

The obligations bind the institution, not the individual, and no act of hers can waive them, because her standing to waive them is the institution's product. They are a floor, not a complete theory of legitimacy. I apply them to the case in [whether consent can legitimate Aadhaar](/questions/can-consent-legitimate-aadhaar/).

## Where the argument stops

The argument is developed through one case. Whether other identification systems are sites of constitutive dependence needs case-specific work I have not done, and the argument is restricted by design to systems with the three architectural features: it does not govern language models or recommender systems. I take no stand on the contested figures for Aadhaar-related exclusion, and nothing in the argument depends on them. Whether the loss of discretion at the point of recognition marks a difference in kind or only in degree is the point on which a reader may reasonably part company with me, and I have tried to locate that disagreement precisely.
