Environmental ethics is the branch of ethics that studies the moral relationship between human beings and the natural world: what in nature has moral standing, and what we owe to animals, plants, species, ecosystems and those who will live after us. It is one of my areas of competence.

Richard Routley's last-man thought experiment helped set its agenda. Suppose the last surviving person, knowing he is the last, sets about destroying every remaining living thing. If he does wrong, as Routley held he plainly does, then the value of nature is not exhausted by its value to us.

## Where moral concern is centred

The field has long been organised by a debate over where moral concern should be centred. Anthropocentric views centre it on human beings and their interests. Sentientist views, of which Peter Singer's is the best known, extend it to every creature that can suffer. Biocentric views, such as Paul Taylor's in *Respect for Nature* (1986), extend it to every living thing as the bearer of a good of its own. Ecocentric views, descended from Aldo Leopold's land ethic, place it in ecological wholes: communities, species, ecosystems. Each extension answers a worry about the one before it and raises new ones, about how to weigh the interests it admits and about what grounds the line it draws.

## The dissertation

My M.A. dissertation at the University of Delhi, *Psychocentrism: A Cognitive Reorientation of Environmental Ethics*, supervised by Dr Narmada Pujari, enters that debate. As its title says, it proposes a cognitive reorientation of the field, under the name psychocentrism. I have not published in environmental ethics.

The open questions are the field's own. What feature of a being makes it matter morally in its own right? How should an answer treat beings whose inner lives are uncertain to us? And can whatever grounds concern for an animal also ground concern for a river or a forest, or does concern for wholes need a different footing altogether?
