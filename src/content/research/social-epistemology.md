Social epistemology studies how knowledge is produced, passed on and held among people and through institutions: testimony, trust, expertise, and the chains along which what one person knows comes to be known by another. My specialisation is epistemology, with a focus on testimony and on the social epistemology of AI, which asks what happens to those practices when one of the links is a machine.

Someone asks a chatbot when a treaty was signed, pastes the answer into a group chat, and a friend repeats it in an essay. Each step looks like testimony. In ["Assertion Without a Speaker"](/publications/assertion-without-a-speaker/), which I presented at AI and Knowledge, University of Delhi, in February 2026, I argue that one of them is not, and that this matters for everyone downstream.

## What survives a link in the chain

[Testimonial chains](/glossary/testimonial-chain/) are a social epistemic technology because their links preserve warrant: A tells B, B tells C, and C can end with warrant that traces back to A. A link preserves warrant when it carries a particular assertion with its provenance, as quotation, recording and faithful relay do. A language model does not. Its parameters compress a corpus into distributional constraints and production selects continuations by likelihood under them, so a claim attested in many unreliable sources can be favoured over one attested in a few authoritative ones, and nothing in the output carries the address of a ground. Chains of model-generated text reposted by users do not preserve warrant as chains of human testimony do. They preserve text.

The claim is structural, and it is easily mistaken for a sociological one. Downstream of the model a person can do the instrument-reading work herself and then tell others on her own account. The chain is then composite, an instrument transaction joined to a testimonial one, and her hearers are fine. If she has not done that work, the chain fails at her link, for the ordinary reason that a testifier lacked warrant. What the model's link transmits, in either case, is nothing. I follow this through in [AI and knowledge chains](/questions/ai-and-knowledge-chains/).

## Who stands behind the sentence

The strongest reply treats the model as a proxy, either for the people whose writing trained it or for a deployer who answers for its outputs (Arora 2024). The first fails at the aggregation step just described. The second fails differently. A spokesperson relays a message her principal has authorised and would stand behind; a deployer has not seen the output, does not believe it, and disclaims it in the terms of service. Responsibility for putting a system into the world is not authorship of its sentences.

This bears on trust. Faulkner distinguishes the affective trust a speaker invites, which makes her answerable for what she says, from the predictive trust one can place in any regular process. Reductive accounts of trust in artefacts, on which it is trust in their makers, and functional ones such as Simion and Kelp's, are good accounts of warranted reliance, and they vindicate the instrument stance rather than testimony. The right attitude to a chatbot is calibrated reliance, which is my answer to [whether you should trust ChatGPT](/questions/should-you-trust-chatgpt/). When an informant misleads, a hearer has social remedies: challenge, a demand for grounds, appeal to others. When an instrument misleads, the remedies are engineering ones. Follow-up questions look like the first kind, but the answers come from the same process as the original and are not independent evidence about it.

## What remains open

The [epistemic opacity](/glossary/epistemic-opacity/) of these systems is a familiar problem, and computational reliabilists such as Durán and Formanek have shown how reliance on opaque processes can be warranted through validation and a track record. I count them allies. What my argument adds is that validation here is not a formality: the record a user projects is all the connection there is.

Two questions seem to me open. Hearers cannot ordinarily tell a composite chain from a simple one, which is why the structural point has practical consequences; what norms of attribution would let them tell? And Freiman's concept of [AI testimony](/glossary/ai-testimony/) picks out a category of outputs without settling whether warrant passes through them. If a system came to meet the Tracking Minimum in some domain, how would anyone downstream know which of its outputs came from that domain?
