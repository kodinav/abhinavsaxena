Indian philosophy is the family of philosophical traditions that developed in the Indian subcontinent, among them Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta and the Buddhist and Jaina schools, with a long and technical literature on knowledge, language and the self. Classical Indian philosophy is one of my wider interests. I read it as a philosopher of testimony rather than as a specialist, and the part of it closest to my work is the theory of [śabda](/glossary/sabda/), knowledge from words.

## Śabda and the reliable speaker

The Nyāya school counted four means of knowledge (*pramāṇa*): perception, inference, comparison and testimony. The *Nyāya-sūtra* defines testimony as the instruction of an *āpta*, a reliable speaker, and Vātsyāyana's commentary characterises the āpta as someone who knows the matter directly and speaks from a wish to convey it as known. The Buddhist epistemologists Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, and the Vaiśeṣika school, treated testimony instead as a form of inference. The dispute is close kin to the modern one between reductionists and non-reductionists about testimony.

What strikes me is where Nyāya puts the weight. Much modern epistemology asks first what entitles the hearer to believe; Nyāya asks what the speaker must be. That is the question I ask of language models in ["Assertion Without a Speaker"](/publications/assertion-without-a-speaker/): not whether a hearer may accept what a source says, but what must be true of the source for knowledge to pass through it. The answers differ in strength. The āpta is defined by knowledge and the intention to convey it, while the [Tracking Minimum](/glossary/tracking-minimum/) is deliberately weaker, asking only that the truth, or the evidence, figure in the explanation of the assertion through the source's own production. On the natural reading every āpta meets it, so a source that fails it is no āpta.

## Words without a speaker

The classical tradition also argued over a case close to the one in my paper's title. Mīmāṃsā held that the Veda has no author, and that its authority is intrinsic because no speaker's faults can enter it; Nyāya replied that its authority derives from the reliability of its author. I do not think either side settles the question about machines, whose outputs have a cause with failure modes of its own. But the shape of the dispute, whether words can carry knowledge when no one stands behind them, is the shape of mine, and I find it useful to read the two together. The comparative essays collected in Matilal and Chakrabarti's *Knowing from Words* (1994) are a good place to begin.
