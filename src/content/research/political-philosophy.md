Political philosophy studies the justification of political power and institutions: what makes authority legitimate, what members of a polity owe one another, and what institutions owe the people they govern. Legitimacy is the question that brings me to it.

Consider a child in India whose first presentation to the welfare state is her Aadhaar enrolment. She does not consent to it, and her parents' proxy consent is not authorisation in the full sense. Yet her standing as a recipient of the mid-day meal, and later as a scholarship holder and an applicant for examinations, is produced through it. Liberal theory traces an institution's legitimacy, through a chain of authorisations, back to individuals whose standing comes first. Her case shows an institution that does not presuppose the standing of those it governs but produces it.

## Legitimacy without authorisation

In ["Beyond Consent"](/publications/beyond-consent/) I argue that liberal frameworks, sophisticated variants included, are frameworks of authorisation: they ask who has standing to authorise what, under what conditions. Where an institution produces the operative standing that authorisation would need, the reasoning turns circular. The institution is legitimated by the subject's authorisation, and the authorising subject is the institution's product. Relational theories of autonomy grant that the self is socially constituted, but they treat that constitution as the background to what she can authorise, not as the thing under evaluation. Pettit's republicanism comes closest, since it too refuses to let formal consent settle legitimacy. But domination is a relation to an agent whose standing is presupposed, and [constitutive dependence](/glossary/constitutive-dependence/) lies upstream of it. There is [a short account of the concept](/questions/what-is-constitutive-dependence/) among my questions.

The positive account draws on Onora O'Neill's case for the priority of obligations, which say who must do what, over rights, and on a single move from Sen: famine analysed as entitlement failure, in which people starve because the structure through which they could claim food has failed. The [Constitutive Obligation Principle](/glossary/constitutive-obligation-principle/) holds that an institution which produces its subjects' operative standing owes them obligations of legibility, non-abandonment and remediation, and that its legitimacy depends on meeting them. They are non-waivable, because the subject's standing to waive them is itself the institution's product. Nor is the principle paternalist. It applies only where authorisation is unavailable in the first place, and it requires that the people governed have a voice in how the obligations are met, while denying that their silence licenses a breach.

## Recognition, legibility and the right to have rights

I locate the condition in the recognition-theoretic tradition, as a distinctively infrastructural form of [misrecognition](/glossary/misrecognition/). Honneth's sphere of legal recognition, Fraser's status model and Butler's claim that recognisability comes before recognition all bear on it. Yet a worn fingerprint that fails to authenticate expresses no cultural devaluation. It is a body the architecture cannot read, and the failure lowers a person's status as surely as any pattern of disesteem. James C. Scott analysed [legibility](/glossary/legibility/) as a technique by which states make populations visible for administration; here legibility has become the medium through which rights are held.

Arendt's stateless person had lost the right to have rights, cast outside every polity. The person in constitutive dependence is inside a polity that acknowledges her in law. What she lacks is recognition at the point where the right must be exercised, and the gap between holding a right and being recognised as its bearer is opened, and administered, by the institution itself.

## What is still open

Extending the concept beyond Aadhaar is a programme, not a result. Integrated identity systems such as Kenya's Huduma Namba and Nigeria's National Identification Number programme are candidate cases. Automated eligibility and fraud-detection systems such as the Dutch SyRI and Australia's Robodebt act on people whose standing they presuppose rather than produce: a neighbouring wrong, on which the vocabulary of domination and the obligation of remediation bear together. I claim that each of the three obligations is necessary, not that together they exhaust what such an institution owes. A further paper, "The Legitimacy of Foundation Models", is under review.
