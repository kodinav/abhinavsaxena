Philosophy of mind studies the nature of mental states, such as belief, perception, understanding and consciousness, and how they relate to behaviour, to the body and to the physical world. It is a wider interest of mine rather than an area I have published in, and I come to it from epistemology.

For most of its history the field had one kind of case: us. Systems that produce fluent language give it a second, and second cases are where concepts show their seams.

## Belief-like states in language models

Whether a language model has anything like beliefs is now partly an empirical question. Herrmann and Levinstein propose standards an internal representation must meet to count as belief-like: accuracy, coherence, uniformity across domains, and use, which requires that the representation actually govern what the model outputs. Probing studies have found structure in models that correlates with truth, but use is the least established of the four. It is also the one that matters for whether a system can be a source of knowledge, which is why my work in epistemology keeps arriving at it. I find the proposal useful because it turns an old question, what it is for a state to be a belief, into one that can be checked against a particular system.

## Understanding, and what my argument leaves alone

Understanding is a separate question. On Williamson's externalist account, to understand an expression is to take part with sufficient fluency in the practice of a linguistic community that uses it, and on that account whether language models understand what they produce is a serious and open question. I do not deny that they may. Nor would a positive answer settle what matters for testimony: understanding is compatible with every epistemic failing the knowledge norm of assertion marks, and a human bullshitter understands his sentences perfectly.

Keeping the questions apart is a matter of method. The condition I defend in ["Assertion Without a Speaker"](/publications/assertion-without-a-speaker/) asks nothing about consciousness, intentions or belief, so its verdict on machines does not wait on questions this field has not settled. An argument that turns on what is constitutively human would lapse the day these systems came to be describable in intentional terms, without anything having been learned about what they do.

Where mind and knowledge meet, one question bears on both: whether the truth-correlated states interpretability finds are used by a model when no one is intervening. Nothing I have argued depends on a view about consciousness in machines.
