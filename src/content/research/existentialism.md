Existentialism is the philosophical tradition that starts from human existence as it is lived, and treats the self not as a fixed nature but as something made through choice, under conditions one did not choose. Its central figures include Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Sartre and Beauvoir. It is a wider interest of mine; I have published nothing in it, and what follows describes why I read it rather than a position I defend.

## Refusal and the standpoint it needs

The existentialists wrote at length about freedom and refusal. Sartre, in his early work, held that even under the harshest constraint a person remains free in how she takes up her situation. Beauvoir gave more weight to situations that close off the possibilities through which freedom is exercised.

My work on consent turns on a related but narrower point. In ["Beyond Consent"](/publications/beyond-consent/) I distinguish coercion from [constitutive dependence](/glossary/constitutive-dependence/). The bandit who demands your money or your life leaves you a dreadful choice, and your refusal, however costly, is still intelligible as yours. An institution that produces your standing as an entitlement-bearer leaves you no such standpoint, because refusing it means refusing the standing from which you would refuse. The argument is about institutions and rests on no existentialist premise. But the existentialists are the philosophers who thought hardest about what it is to have, or to lack, a standpoint from which to say no.

## Selves and systems

The other question I take from the tradition concerns technologies that anticipate, recommend and decide on our behalf. The question is not whether they are useful, since many plainly are, but what they do to the task of choosing: whether a choice that has been made easy is still, in the relevant sense, mine, and what it means to answer for a self that such systems helped to shape. I have not worked this out, and I keep it among the questions I read with rather than the claims I make.
