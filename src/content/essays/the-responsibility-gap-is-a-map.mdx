When a learning system causes harm, who is to blame? The question has a standard shape in AI ethics, and a standard worry attached to it. Andreas Matthias named the worry the responsibility gap. Systems that learn behave in ways their designers did not specify and could not predict in detail. Blame requires control and knowledge. The designers lacked both with respect to the particular outcome; the users lacked them; and the system is not the kind of thing that can be blamed. So responsibility, it seems, falls into a gap.

I think the worry describes something real and misdescribes its shape. Responsibility in these cases does not disappear. It moves, and spreads, and the task is to draw where it has gone accurately enough to act on.

## How we already handle machines

The gap looks most alarming if we picture responsibility as a quantity that must be located in a single place. Our practice with complex technologies has never worked that way. Helen Nissenbaum showed decades ago how accountability in computerised systems is spread across the many hands that design, build, deploy and operate them, and how that spreading can be an excuse for no one answering for anything. Her point was not that responsibility vanishes but that we let it diffuse.

We already have workable practices for instruments that no one controls in every particular. We hold the makers of certified instruments answerable for how their instruments behave. We regulate where and how they may be used. We expect users to rely on them competently and blame them when they do not. None of these parties controls each outcome, and none is excused for that reason. A bridge that fails in an unforeseen way is not an occasion for concluding that no one is responsible for bridges.

## What is new

Learning systems strain these practices without breaking them. They redistribute control and knowledge in unfamiliar ways. The designer controls the training process but not the behaviour it produces in a given case; the deployer controls the setting but not the system's internal workings; the user controls the question but not the answer. Harms can arise from interactions that no single party could reasonably have foreseen.

That makes the map harder to draw. It does not empty it. Designers answer for the systems they release and for what they claim about them. Deployers answer for putting a system where its failures fall on people who cannot absorb them. Overseers answer for the checks they do or do not run. Users answer for relying competently, in proportion to what they could have known about how far to rely.

## What a system cannot be held to

There is one thing the map should not contain, though it is tempting to add it. Some philosophers propose that a system itself can bear responsibility, functionally: a system built to perform a task can be criticised for failing at it. That criticism is fair, and useful. But it describes a failure; it does not create a new bearer of blame who could stand in for the people who are missing.

In my work on AI testimony I put the related point in terms of authorship. When a language model produces a false answer, its deployer is answerable for having put it into the world. But the deployer has not seen the answer, does not believe it and would disclaim it. That is not the relation of a principal to a message her spokesperson relays. Responsibility for putting a system into the world is not authorship of its sentences, and treating it as authorship is one way of pretending the gap is closed when it is merely relabelled.

## Reading the map

So I would rather speak of a map than a hole. A hole invites despair or denial: either responsibility is lost, or the gap is imaginary. A map invites the work that needs doing. Where, for this system and this harm, did control sit, and knowledge? Who could have foreseen what, and who chose to deploy it where its failures would land on others? Where did reliance outrun what anyone was entitled to?

Those questions have answers, and the answers can be written into law, into design practice and into the habits of the people who rely on these systems. The responsibility gap names the places where our existing answers run out. It is an invitation to draw further, not a reason to stop.
