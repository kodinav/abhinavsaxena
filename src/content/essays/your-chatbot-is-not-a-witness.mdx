A witness is someone whose word can make you know. You were not there; she was. She tells you what she saw, and if she is competent and sincere, you now know it too, on her say-so. Most of what any of us knows we know in this way: the date of a treaty, the shape of a continent, the dose of a medicine. Philosophers call it testimony, and they have spent a long time asking what makes it work.

Chatbots answer questions in the grammar of testimony. They say that the treaty was signed in a certain year, in the same declarative voice a historian would use. And they are often right. So it is natural to treat them as witnesses: as sources whose word, like a person's, can make you know. I think this is a mistake, and not because they are often wrong. The mistake would remain if they were never wrong.

## What makes a word worth taking

Begin with a case that has nothing to do with machines. A thermometer hangs in a room whose temperature a thermostat keeps exactly constant. The thermometer is broken: its needle is stuck, and would read the same whatever the temperature. As it happens, it is stuck at the right number. Its readings are correct, and correct for a stable, explicable reason. Suppose someone posts them every hour. Does anyone who reads the posts come to know the temperature?

No. And the reason is instructive. The reading is right, but the temperature plays no part in why it reads as it does. Change the temperature and the reading would not move. The accuracy is real, but it is borrowed from the thermostat, not earned by the thermometer.

Read carefully, the leading philosophical accounts of testimony agree on a condition that explains the verdict. Burge, Williamson, Goldberg, Faulkner and Lackey disagree about a great deal, but each requires, in his or her own terms, that the truth of what a source asserts, or the evidence for it, figure in the explanation of why the source asserts it, and figure there through the source's own operation rather than through some proxy. I call this the [Tracking Minimum](/questions/tracking-minimum/). The broken thermometer fails it, and so does any source whose accuracy, however impressive, is borrowed.

## Two kinds of reliable

There are, then, two things we might mean by calling a source reliable. One is a high rate of true outputs under the conditions it is used in. The other is that its outputs would follow the truth if the truth were different. The first is a fact about a track record; the second, about what the source is responsive to. The broken thermometer is perfectly reliable in the first sense and not at all in the second.

Testimony needs the second. Lackey's well-known case of the creationist biology teacher shows that it does not need belief: the teacher does not believe the evolutionary theory she teaches, yet her students, on her word, come to know it. But notice why. If an error crept into her textbook, she would catch it and decline to teach it; if the evidence were different, she would teach differently. Her production of assertions follows the evidence as far as her competence reaches. She lacks the belief; she does not lack the connection.

## Where the chatbot stands

What governs a large language model's answer to an open question? On the best current description, the answer is selected because it is likely given the patterns the model learned from an enormous body of human writing, adjusted by training against human approval. Where what is likely to be written and what is true coincide, which in well-covered domains is very often, the answer is right. The truth reaches it through a proxy: the fact that true things tend to be what gets written down.

Hold that proxy fixed and vary the truth, and the answer does not move. That is the thermometer's situation, at a vastly larger scale and with vastly more practical value. It is why I think current systems, on the best current evidence, do not transmit testimonial knowledge, however often they are right.

The friendly evidence deserves to be stated, because it is real. Researchers have found structure inside these models that correlates with the truth of what they represent, and intervening on it can make their answers more truthful. That shows the structure is a lever. It does not show that the lever is what steers when no one is pulling it, and that is what the Tracking Minimum asks about. Nor is the argument an argument from inner life. I do not claim that these systems lack understanding, intentions or minds; the condition at issue is third-personal, and a machine could in principle meet it. Some do, in narrow domains where their training checks answers against a verifier. Open-ended conversation is not such a domain.

## What you are doing instead

If the chatbot is not a witness, what is it? I think the right model is the instrument. When you read a barometer, nothing is told to you. You come to know the pressure because the instrument is reliable in conditions like these, you know how to read it, and you have no reason to doubt it. The knowledge is yours, generated by your use of the instrument, and so is the responsibility for using it well.

That is the structure of relying on a chatbot. When it goes well, you know because you had good reason to think the system reliable on questions like yours and nothing in the answer gave you reason for doubt. The work of judging that was yours, whether or not you noticed doing it.

There is a sting in the comparison. A working barometer meets the Tracking Minimum: vary the pressure and the needle moves, in virtue of the instrument's own mechanism. The chatbot, on the best current evidence, does not. So it is not merely an instrument whose calibration you cannot see; in respect of the particular content it emits, it is not calibrated to anything. The record you project from past successes to this answer is all the connection there is.

## Why the label matters

It might seem that nothing practical turns on whether we call the chatbot a witness or an instrument, so long as it is usually right. Three things turn on it.

First, deference. A witness lets you defer: the warrant runs through her, and you may rest on it. An instrument does not. Treating a chatbot's answer as testimony invites you to stop checking at exactly the point where checking is your job.

Second, remedies. When a witness misleads you, you can challenge her, ask for her grounds, compare her with others who know her. When an instrument misleads you, the remedies are engineering ones. A chatbot's conversational manner invites the first kind of response, but its answers to your follow-up questions come from the same process as the answer you are questioning, so they are not independent evidence about it.

Third, chains. Testimony travels: a historian tells a journalist, the journalist tells you. Because a chatbot preserves no one's grounds, chains of its answers, reposted and repeated, [do not carry knowledge](/essays/they-preserve-text/) the way chains of testimony do.

None of this makes the chatbot useless. Instruments are among the great sources of human knowledge. It makes it something other than what its grammar suggests. A practice that mistakes such an instrument for a speaker is in trouble in proportion to how heavily the instrument is used, and an accurate description does its own corrective work.
