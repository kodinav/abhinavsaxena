An elderly woman who has worked the fields all her life goes to the ration shop for her month's grain. The shop authenticates entitlement by fingerprint. Decades of manual labour have worn the ridges of her fingers smooth, and the scanner does not recognise them. She tries again. It still does not. She goes home without her rations.

The usual way to describe what went wrong is in the language of consent and data protection. Did she agree to biometric authentication? Was she told how her data would be used, and what alternatives she had? Could she have opted out? These questions organise the Aadhaar Act's own provisions, the Supreme Court's judgments, India's Data Protection Act of 2023, and much of the activism and scholarship that has grown up around the system's failures. I think they are the wrong questions for her case, and seeing why tells us something about what algorithmic welfare systems are.

## Not a failure to consent

She is not failing to give informed consent. She is failing to be recognised by the system as someone who could give or withhold consent to anything at all. Her relationship to Aadhaar is not that of a rights-bearer, already standing, who is then asked to authorise the processing of her data. Aadhaar is the condition under which she becomes visible to the welfare state as a rights-bearer in the first place. She cannot refuse the system without refusing the standing from which a refusal would make sense.

This needs stating carefully, because it is easy to hear it as something absurd. She is, of course, a person, a citizen and a legal subject whatever any database says, and her human rights are not the creation of an identity authority. What the system produces is narrower and, for her purposes, decisive: her operative standing at a particular counter, the standing in virtue of which she counts there as someone to whom grain is owed and is handed over. When the fingerprint fails, that standing fails with it.

I call this condition [constitutive dependence](/questions/what-is-constitutive-dependence/). A person stands in it when an institution produces her operative recognition as a bearer of entitlements rather than merely administering a recognition that exists independently of it; when she has no standpoint from which to refuse or correct it without giving up the standing she would be protecting; and when the institution can revise her recognition through its own operations, without appeal to an independent ground.

## Why consent cannot do the work

The liberal tradition asks four things of valid consent. The person must be competent, informed and acting voluntarily, and she must have standing over the matter, the kind of standing that makes her authorisation count. Debates about consent going wrong almost always concern the first three: the patient without capacity, the user who did not read the policy, the worker who cannot refuse surveillance. The fourth is taken for granted.

Her case fails on the fourth. For a documented, urban citizen, refusing to enrol is at least imaginable: she can weigh the costs of living outside the system and decline them. For a rural labourer without a birth certificate or a stable address, the standpoint of refusal is not merely costly but illusory. Refusing Aadhaar is not refusing an identification method; it is refusing to exist as someone the welfare state can see. That is not a high price for an option. It is a category mistake about what kind of option is on offer.

This distinguishes two ways consent can fail. Sometimes it fails practically: its conditions could be met but are not, as when a privacy policy is too long to read, and better design is the fix. Sometimes it fails conceptually: no redesign, however ambitious, could restore it. Medical ethics already recognises the second kind. When a patient lacks the capacity to consent, we do not print a clearer leaflet; we turn to surrogate decision and best interests. Constitutive dependence calls for a comparable shift, not because the people affected lack capacity, but because the standing that consent presupposes is what the system produces.

## Three faces of the same structure

The woman at the ration shop is one face of it. A second is the migrant worker. Her entitlement to subsidised grain has historically been tied to a particular shop in a particular district, so the movement on which her livelihood depends puts her legibility, and with it her food, at risk. Schemes for national portability help many and miss others, especially those whose documents are incomplete at one end or the other. The system does not record a geography of entitlement that exists independently of it. It makes one.

A third is the child. A child enrolled in Aadhaar before she can agree to anything has her standing as a recipient of school meals and scholarships produced, in part, through the enrolment itself. No proxy consent by her parents can be authorisation in the full sense. What the child shows openly is what is true, less visibly, of every adult the system governs.

None of these depends on any contested number. Estimates of how often authentication fails vary across surveys and are disputed by the authority itself, and I take no stand on their magnitude. The argument needs only what is not seriously in dispute: that failure is patterned rather than random, concentrated among people whose hands carry the marks of the labour the welfare system exists to protect, and that for those it excludes the consequences can be grave.

## What such a system owes

If consent cannot legitimate a system that produces its subjects' standing, something else must. My proposal, which I call the Constitutive Obligation Principle, is that its legitimacy depends on discharging obligations that its subjects cannot waive, because waiving would itself be a form of the authorisation that is not available. There are at least three, each answering to a feature of the dependence.

**Legibility.** Because the institution produces recognition, it cannot treat its criteria as natural facts beyond its responsibility. A fingerprint criterion that systematically fails the hands of manual workers is a choice, and the institution owes its subjects criteria that fit the material reality of their lives.

**Non-abandonment.** Because the institution can revise recognition through its own operations, it may not withdraw what it produced without meeting a burden of justification. A cancelled card or a failed database link is not a clerical event. It is a withdrawal of recognition.

**Remediation.** Because its subjects have no standpoint outside it, they must have a substantive pathway inside it when recognition fails. Sending the woman whose fingerprints failed to an online grievance portal discharges no genuine obligation. It redoubles the failure.

## Not an outlier

It would be comforting to treat Aadhaar as a peculiarity of one country's administration. I think it is an early instance of a form towards which algorithmic governance is converging: automated decisions, criteria enforced without the discretion by which an official can recognise someone manifestly entitled, and entitlements conditioned on records held and cross-checked elsewhere. Wherever those three features come together, some people's standing will be produced by the system rather than presupposed by it. For them, the question is not whether they agreed. It is what the system owes them, and whether it is paying.
