Legibility, in James C. Scott's sense, is a state's capacity to make its population and territory readable for administration. In *Seeing Like a State* Scott traced how states imposed permanent surnames, cadastral maps, standard weights and measures and population registers, simplifying complex local practices into forms a central administration could record, tax and conscript. His point was double: legibility is a precondition of much that states do, welfare included, and the simplifications it requires can fail, sometimes disastrously, the people they are meant to describe. Torpey's history of the passport and Breckenridge's history of biometric registration in South Africa carry the story into identification.

I extend Scott's term in two ways. The first is descriptive. In algorithmic welfare systems legibility is no longer only a technique by which the state sees its population; it becomes the operative medium of rights-bearing itself. A labourer whose fingerprint does not read is not merely invisible to an administrator. At the point of transaction she is not, operatively, a person to whom the ration is owed. That is the condition I call [constitutive dependence](/glossary/constitutive-dependence/).

The second is normative. Under the [Constitutive Obligation Principle](/glossary/constitutive-obligation-principle/), an institution that produces recognition owes its subjects legibility: criteria of recognition responsive to the material reality of the people it must reach. It may not treat its criteria as exogenous natural facts. If a readable fingerprint is the criterion and it systematically fails those whose labour has worn their fingers, the obligation is to redesign the criterion. Nothing requires perfect biometrics. What is required is that the institution treat its subjects' legibility as a substantive achievement for which it is responsible, not a background condition it can take for granted.

The two uses fit together. Scott showed what states gain by making people legible. The obligation of legibility says what an institution owes once it has made being legible the condition of a person's standing: that its criteria of being seen answer to the lives of those who must be seen.
