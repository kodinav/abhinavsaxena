Zhuangzi watches the minnows darting below the bridge and says that this is the pleasure of fishes. His friend Huizi, a logician, objects: you are not a fish, so how can you know what fish enjoy? Zhuangzi turns the objection back on him, and then, when Huizi presses, he returns to the original question. You asked me how I knew, he says, which shows you knew that I knew; and in Giles's rendering he ends: 'I knew it from my own feelings on this bridge.' Giles adds a gloss of his own, that from his feelings above the bridge Zhuangzi infers those of the fishes below.

The passage is often read as a triumph of intuition over logic, or as a joke at the logician's expense. I read it as a dispute about the source of a claim to know. Huizi treats knowledge of another mind as requiring something like being that mind. Zhuangzi locates his knowledge in where he stood and what he was looking at. Giles's gloss makes the knowledge an inference from his own case, which is one way of filling in the answer; the text itself leaves more open.

That relational picture is close to the one I defend for testimony: what matters is not whether the knower shares the nature of what is known, but whether the truth of the matter figures, through the knower's own engagement with it, in why the claim is made. It also says something about the questions people ask of AI. Whether a system has an inner life is the question Huizi would ask. What its outputs are responsive to is the question Zhuangzi's answer suggests.
